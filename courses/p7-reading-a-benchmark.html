Reading a benchmark

Sessions 5 and 6 were about a set you own. Tonight the set belongs to somebody else, the number has already been published, and the only decision left is how much of it to carry across to your own problem. The answer is less than it looks, and it is not zero. The procedure takes four questions and ends in three written sentences you attach to the citation, so that anyone reading your deck next quarter can see exactly what the rank was asked to support.

Part 0

Where tonight sits

Session 5 measured what selecting on your own set costs, and session 6 found the lever that controls it. Both assumed you were holding the set. A published rank is the same object one step further away: somebody else chose the task, somebody else collected the data, somebody else decided what counts as an error, and none of those three people had your problem in mind. Session 8 goes one level up again, to a body of evidence rather than a single number.

Live = written together in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A four-question pass you can run against any leaderboard in about ten minutes, before the number reaches a slide. The reason a rank belongs to a pairing of method and benchmark rather than to the method. The one piece of measured evidence that says which half of a benchmark result travels and which half does not. And the artifact: three sentences that say what the rank licenses for your problem, what it does not, and what you would have to measure yourself to close the difference.

LiveA benchmark is an instrument, and construct validity is the question4 min▶

Every benchmark returns a number about a specific quantity, under specific conditions, using a specific rule for what counts as wrong. Construct validity asks whether that quantity is the one you care about. It is a different question from whether the reading is accurate, and it is the one that usually goes unasked, because an accurate reading of the wrong quantity looks exactly like an accurate reading.

The practical form of the question splits into three, and every leaderboard row answers all three silently. What task was formalised. Whose data it was computed on. How predictions were converted into one number. A rank is a claim about a method paired with those three things, and the pairing is what does not come with you when you copy the number into your own document.

Self-studyThe words that will keep coming back4 min▶
  • Construct validity. Whether the quantity an instrument measures is the quantity you intended to measure. Precision and reproducibility say nothing about it.
  • Task. The decision the benchmark formalises: what is predicted, from what inputs, at which moment.
  • Distribution. Whose data, collected when, by what process, and who is not in it.
  • Scoring rule. How a set of predictions becomes one number, which is also the decision about which direction of error is allowed to matter.
  • Task set. The collection of benchmarks a community happens to use. It is a choice, and it participates in the result.
  • Level and ordering. How well a method scores, against whether it scores better than another one. They do not travel equally well, and most citations carry the wrong one.
  • The licence. The written statement of what a published number supports for your problem. Three sentences, attached to the citation.
Part 1 · covers construct validity

Three questions hiding inside one rank

12 min live

Run these against the leaderboard before the number reaches anybody senior. Each one takes a sentence to answer and a sentence to answer for your own problem, and the two sentences side by side are most of the finding.

LiveWhy an accurate reading is the trap4 min▶

Published benchmark numbers are usually computed correctly. The code is often released, the protocol is documented, and a second team running it gets the same figure. All of that is evidence about precision and none of it is evidence about which quantity was measured. A thermometer that reads to two decimal places is still the wrong instrument for measuring a room's humidity, and nobody catches that by checking the decimal places.

The failure shows up in a particular way: the team that cites the number is not wrong about the number, so the conversation never lands on anything checkable. Naming the three parts converts it into something checkable. Once the benchmark's task is written as one sentence next to yours, disagreement becomes possible, and disagreement that can be settled is worth far more than agreement that cannot.

Real world

A vendor's model leads a public clinical prediction benchmark. Thornbury predicts deterioration within 24 hours, on a district group's intake, for a decision where missing a deteriorating patient and pulling a nurse to a stable one cost very different amounts. If the benchmark's outcome window, intake and error pricing differ on any of those three, the lead is still real and it is a lead on a different measurement.

Self-studyThe fourth question, which is about you3 min▶

After the three, there is a fourth that nobody else can answer: what would this method score on our data. It is the only question whose answer settles an absolute claim, and the reason to ask it last is that the first three tell you how large the gap you are trying to close is likely to be. A benchmark whose task, distribution and scoring rule are all close to yours needs a small confirmation. One that differs on all three needs a measurement, not a confirmation.

Asking it also changes what a supplier conversation is about. A rank is not falsifiable by you. A figure computed on a set you kept clean is, which turns a sales claim into a testable one at the cost of some calendar time and no statistics at all.

Part 2 · covers the benchmark lottery

The rank has three inputs and only one is the method

9 min live

Dehghani, Tay, Gritsenko, Zhao, Houlsby, Diaz, Metzler and Vinyals gave the effect a name in 2021. Nobody has to cheat for it to operate, and understanding why is what stops a practitioner from over-reading a lead.

LiveWhat the word lottery does and does not mean4 min▶

It does not mean the results are random, and it does not mean anybody gamed anything. It means the set of tasks a community settled on is itself a choice, made before the methods existed and usually for reasons of history rather than design. That choice is part of the answer. A method that suits the tasks in the set will place well in it, and placing well in it is what the word leading means in practice.

The practitioner consequence is a counting habit rather than a rule. One benchmark is one instrument reading. If a lead exists on one task set, the claim has survived nothing yet. If it holds across several unlike task sets, it has survived something and the ordering is worth carrying into a shortlist. If the lead exists on exactly the benchmark the authors are best known for, say that out loud when you cite it.

Self-studyWhy this is not a reason to stop using benchmarks3 min▶

A shared benchmark is how a field makes progress comparable at all. Without one, every claim is a demonstration and nothing can be ranked against anything. Reading the lottery as a reason to discard published evidence replaces a calibrated inference with no inference, which is a worse position to argue from and much harder to correct.

The useful reading is about the strength of an inference rather than its existence. A rank is real evidence of modest strength about a comparison, and the strength rises with the number of unlike task sets the comparison survives. That is a dial, not a switch, and treating it as a switch is how teams end up choosing models on vibes after deciding the numbers cannot be trusted.

Part 3 · covers what transfers

The level fell and the ordering held

9 min live

Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt and Shankar built genuinely new test sets and scored the existing models on them. The result has two halves and they point in opposite directions, which is why the retellings usually keep one.

LiveThree results that pull against each other, kept apart5 min▶

The intuitive explanation for a fresh test set producing worse numbers is that the old one had been scored against so often that the community had quietly fitted to it. Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt and Shankar concluded otherwise for these drops: the cause was the models' limited ability to generalise to slightly harder images rather than contamination through repeated use. Roelofs, Shankar and colleagues went at the same suspicion from another angle, across over one hundred Kaggle competitions where entrants score repeatedly against a public holdout and a separate set decides the final ranking, and found little evidence of substantial adaptive overfitting.

Against that, session 5 measured the mechanism directly at Thornbury and it is real: optimism of 0.0027 at one candidate rising to 0.0072 at fifty, then flat. Small, and not zero. Three findings, all sound, and the temptation is to collapse them into a single slogan in one direction or the other. Keeping them apart is the skill. Reuse is a real mechanism with a modest measured cost. The distribution gap is a separate and larger effect. A published number can be hurt by both and they need different responses.

Real world

Two teams argue about a leaderboard. One says the rankings are meaningless because everyone has overfitted the benchmark. The other says the numbers are fine because a well-run leaderboard uses a hidden set. Neither position survives the evidence: the reuse effect is measurable and small, and the much larger fall in the ImageNet and CIFAR-10 work came from somewhere else entirely. The question that moves the argument forward is which of the two is operating here, and that is answerable.

Self-studyWhat a bigger fall would and would not tell you3 min▶

Suppose you rebuild a benchmark's test set yourself, following its collection process, and your models score several points lower. That fall is consistent with two very different stories: the community fitted to the old set, or your rebuild is slightly harder than the original in ways nobody intended. Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt and Shankar ran into exactly that ambiguity and landed on the second explanation.

The observation that separates them is the ordering. If the community had fitted to the old set, the methods that had been tuned hardest against it would fall furthest and the ranking would scramble. If the new set is simply harder, everything falls together and the ranking survives. The ranking surviving, plus gains on the original sets translating to larger gains on the new ones, is what made the second story the better fit. That is a diagnostic you can run yourself whenever you build a second test set.

Part 4 · covers the output

Write the licence before you cite the number

10 min live

The whole pass produces one artifact, and it is three sentences long. It goes next to the citation, in the same document, so that the reader six months from now can see what the rank was asked to carry.

Name the instrument. Which benchmark, by name, which version, and who built it. A claim whose instrument cannot be named is not a measurement and the pass stops here.

Write the two task sentences. The benchmark's task and yours, same shape, same length: what is predicted, from what inputs, at which moment. Put them on consecutive lines and read them out loud.

Write the two distribution sentences. Whose data and when, for both. Add the clause that usually decides it: who is in theirs that is not in yours, and who is in yours that is not in theirs.

Ask what the scoring rule forgives. Which direction of error it prices cheaply, and who pays for that error in your setting. If the two answers differ, the rank is measuring a trade-off your operation does not make.

Count the unlike task sets the lead survives. One is a reading. Two unlike ones is a claim. If the lead exists on exactly one benchmark, write that sentence into the citation rather than leaving the reader to discover it.

Write the three sentences and attach them. Licenses, does not license, would close the gap. In the same document as the number, not in a separate review that nobody opens.

Turn the third sentence into a task with an owner. A measurement nobody is scheduled to run is a sentence, not a plan, and the number will be cited unqualified by the second meeting.

★ The licence, filled in for a Thornbury shortlist CLAIM AS RECEIVED "Ranked 1st on <benchmark name>, accuracy <figure>." INSTRUMENT Benchmark: <name, version, who built it> Task: predicts <what> from <inputs> at <moment> Ours: predicts deterioration within 24 hours from vitals drift and lab trend, at admission Distribution: <whose data, collected when, by what process> Ours: Thornbury district intake, deployment year, wards already reorganised Scoring rule: <what counts as wrong, and how much> Ours: a missed deterioration and a pulled nurse do not cost the same LICENCE Licenses: probably the better of the two methods listed, on that benchmark's task and distribution. Does not: any accuracy figure for Thornbury patients. The level did not transfer in the one place anybody measured it: 3 to 15 percent on CIFAR-10, 11 to 14 percent on ImageNet. To close it: score the candidate on 2,000 Thornbury patients from a set that took no part in the choice. Owner: <name>. By: <date>. LEAD SURVIVES 1 task set. Not yet a claim about the method.

The habit to keep. The licence is written before the number is quoted, not after somebody challenges it. Written first it takes ten minutes and reads as diligence. Written second it takes an hour and reads as a retreat, and by then the unqualified figure is already in three decks and one board pack.

Check yourself

Three questions

A published model leads a clinical benchmark by two points. Which part of that result are you entitled to carry into a Thornbury shortlist?

The one place anybody built genuinely new test sets and measured both halves, the level fell substantially and the ranking largely survived. Carrying the ordering into a shortlist is supported. Carrying the absolute figure into a forecast is not, and discarding the whole thing throws away real comparative evidence.

Two internal teams each cite a different benchmark and reach opposite conclusions about which method is better. What does the benchmark lottery say is going on?

The lottery postulates that factors other than fundamental algorithmic superiority can make a method be perceived as superior, and nobody has to act in bad faith for that. Averaging unlike instruments does not help either. The question to ask is which instrument measures the quantity your decision needs.

A colleague argues that leaderboard rankings are worthless because the community has overfitted the benchmarks. What does the evidence support?

Session 5 measured the reuse mechanism at Thornbury: optimism of 0.0027 at one candidate, 0.0072 at fifty, then flat, so it is real and modest. The two field results looked for large adaptive overfitting where it should have been worst and did not find it, and they attributed the large falls to a different effect. All three are sound and none of them supports the slogan in either direction.
